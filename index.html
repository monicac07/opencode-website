01About BostonHacks

A place to turn “what if?” into a project.

BostonHacks brings students together to build technology projects during a hackathon. Instead of only reading about an idea, participants work on something they can try, explain, and share.

This guide is for students exploring that experience, including beginners. Think of it as a starting point, not an event handbook: the suggestions here are general hackathon guidance, not BostonHacks rules or promises.

Explore

Follow a question

Notice a small problem in your day. Could a tool, visualization, or prototype help you understand it differently?

Experiment

Build a small first version

A focused idea gives you room to learn. One useful feature is a better starting point than a long list you cannot finish.

Connect

Learn with other people

Explain your thinking, ask questions, and compare approaches. Sharing what did not work can be as useful as showing what did.

02Get Involved

Pick a starting point. Take the next step.

These are general preparation suggestions, not BostonHacks participation requirements.

  1. Find your starting point

    Think about what draws you to a hackathon: learning a tool, exploring a problem, or collaborating on a small project. Pick one goal to guide your preparation.

  2. Choose something to learn

    Try a small exercise with a tool that interests you. Bring a question or a rough idea; this guide does not assume you already have a polished project.

  3. Think about collaboration

    Practice introducing your interests and what you want to learn. Ask classmates about their ideas and discuss how your skills could complement each other.

A guide, not an event handbook. This class project shares general preparation ideas. It does not accept registrations, confirm eligibility, or set event policies.

03Frequently Asked Questions

New route? Good questions.

Open a question for general beginner guidance. Event-specific answers should always come from the organizers.

What is a hackathon?

A hackathon is a time-limited gathering for making projects, often with technology and collaboration at its center. The “hack” usually means creative building, not breaking into computer systems. Formats vary, so check the event’s own description.

Do I need programming experience?

You can start exploring project ideas before you feel expert. Research, design, explaining a problem, and testing a prototype can all support a project. This is general encouragement, not a statement about BostonHacks eligibility or available support.

How can I find teammates?

Start by sharing what you are interested in and what you hope to learn. Ask classmates about their ideas, and look for complementary interests rather than identical skills. This guide does not specify BostonHacks team rules or promise team-finding activities.

How should I prepare?

Practice opening and saving a small project with a tool you want to use. Write down a manageable idea and a few questions. Give yourself a chance to experiment before worrying about a polished result. This is general learning advice, not an event packing list.

What is a manageable first project?

Choose one small question and one thing to build around it. You might sketch an app screen, visualize a small dataset, or make a simple interactive page. These are practice ideas, not BostonHacks tracks or submission requirements.
